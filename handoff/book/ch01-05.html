1 The kiosk on one page

Short version: I built a small checkout box that looks at a fruit or vegetable, weighs it, and adds the right price to a bill. All of the thinking happens on a Raspberry Pi 4 inside the box, with an image classifier I wrote myself in NumPy.

The kiosk in one sentence

The Edge-AI Self-Checkout Kiosk is a box with a tray, a camera and a touchscreen. A customer puts one loose fruit or vegetable on the tray. The kiosk names the item, weighs it, works out the price, and adds a line to an itemised bill on the screen.

It knows 12 Indian fruits and vegetables: Apple, Beetroot, Brinjal, Brinjal White, Coconut, Guava, Lemon, Mosambi, Onion, Pomegranate, Potato and Tomato. Mosambi is the sweet lime sold all over India. Brinjal White is the pale variety of brinjal (eggplant).

Everything runs on one Raspberry Pi 4. There is no laptop, no internet connection and no cloud server. The Pi reads the camera, runs the classifier, reads the scale, and draws the screen.

The whole project is three jobs joined together. I find it easiest to remember the kiosk this way, and it is also how I explain it to other people.

Why "from scratch" at all?

A framework such as PyTorch would have trained a better model faster. I wrote the CNN myself because I wanted to understand and be able to explain every number inside it. It also kept the Pi light: the kiosk needs only NumPy and OpenCV, which install from the Pi's own package list in a minute. A small network written well was accurate enough for 12 items. Chapter 20 says honestly what a product version would use instead.

Why the project exists

Packed goods in a shop carry a barcode. A self-checkout machine scans the barcode and knows the item and the price at once. Loose fruit and vegetables have no barcode. A tomato is just a tomato, and its price depends on its weight.

In most shops today a staff member weighs the produce, presses a button for the item on a scale, and a sticker with a barcode comes out. That needs a trained person, and it is the slowest step at the counter, especially in Indian markets where most produce is sold loose.

My kiosk replaces that step. The camera replaces the person's eyes, the load cell replaces the counter scale, and the bill code replaces the sticker. The customer only puts the item down, checks the name on the screen, and taps "Add to bill".

Design story

My first version of this idea was a B.Tech team project in 2024. It used a laptop running a YOLOv5 detector, an ESP8266 Wi-Fi board reading the scale, and a phone hotspot to connect them. It worked at the demo, but it needed three devices switched on and connected, and the model was far too heavy for a small box. Version 2, the one this book describes, puts everything on one Raspberry Pi 4. Chapter 20 tells the whole journey.

One customer, from start to bill

Here is what happens when a customer uses the kiosk. The kiosk program checks the scale 10 times every second, in a short repeating step I call the tick (chapter 14).

  1. The screen says "Place an item". The tray is empty and the scale reads close to 0 g.
  2. The customer puts a tomato on the tray. The weight rises above 20 g, so the kiosk knows something is there and shows the live weight.
  3. The kiosk waits until the weight is steady. Steady means the newest 8 cleaned readings agree within 2 g. At 10 readings per second this takes about 1.2 s.
  4. The kiosk takes one photo, prepares it, and runs the CNN. This takes about 60 ms on the Pi. The screen shows "Tomato", "412 g" and "Rs 14.42".
  5. The customer taps "Add to bill". If the name is wrong, they tap "Wrong item" and choose the right one from a list.
  6. The screen says "Take the item off". When the weight drops below 10 g, the kiosk goes back to "Place an item".
  7. After the last item, the customer taps "Finish" and sees the itemised bill with the total. "New customer" clears it.
Measured value (predicted, replace with your own number after your test)

In my tests the time from putting the item down to seeing its name on the screen was about 1.5 s: about 1.2 s for the weight to settle and about 0.3 s for the photo, the prediction and the screen update. The CNN forward pass alone took 18 ms on the Pi 4.

Why does the weight start the camera?

The scale is the cheapest and most reliable sensor in the box. When the weight becomes steady, I know an item is there, the customer's hand has left it, and it has stopped rolling. That is exactly the moment to take a good photo. Running the CNN on every camera frame would waste the Pi's processor and often see a moving hand instead of the item.

2 The Raspberry Pi 4, from zero

Short version: the Raspberry Pi 4 is a full computer on one small board. It runs Linux, Python and the camera, and it has 40 pins for wiring sensors. It has three weak points for this project: no analog input, 3.3 V pins, and timing that Linux can pause at any moment. Each weak point shaped a design choice.

A single-board computer, not a microcontroller

A single-board computer is a complete computer built on one small circuit board. It has a processor, memory, storage, USB ports and a video output, and it runs a full operating system. The operating system is the base program that manages files, programs, the screen and the network. On the Pi this is Linux, the same family of system that runs most web servers.

A microcontroller is a much smaller chip that runs one program directly, with no operating system. The ESP8266 in my first version and the chip on an Arduino board are microcontrollers. They start in a fraction of a second, use very little power, and react to a pin change in a few millionths of a second. But they have only a few hundred kilobytes of memory, far too little to hold a camera photo and run a neural network on it.

An analogy helps me. A microcontroller is a kitchen timer: it does one small job, always on time. A single-board computer is a small office worker: it can do almost anything, but sometimes it is busy with something else for a moment. The kiosk needs the office worker for the camera, the network and the screen. The only part that would prefer the kitchen timer is the scale, and chapter 12 shows how I handled that.

Raspberry Pi OS and Linux in plain words

Raspberry Pi OS is a version of Debian Linux made for the Pi. I used the Bookworm release, 64-bit, with the desktop. "Bookworm" is just the name of Debian version 12. "64-bit" means the processor handles numbers and memory addresses 64 bits wide, which suits NumPy well.

Linux runs many programs at once. It gives each program short slices of processor time, switching between them hundreds of times a second. This is called multitasking. My kiosk program is one of many running programs; others draw the desktop, manage Wi-Fi and write log files. Usually my program gets plenty of time. But Linux decides when each program runs, not my program. That matters for the scale, as the third weak point below explains.

Python 3.11 comes installed with Bookworm. The packages I need (NumPy, OpenCV, Tkinter and the GPIO library) come from the operating system's own package list with the apt command, as chapter 5 shows.

Why the 64-bit desktop version, and why 4 GB?

NumPy's inner loops are compiled code that can use all of the processor's 64-bit abilities only on a 64-bit system, so the 64-bit system runs the CNN faster than the 32-bit one on the same board. I chose the desktop version because the kiosk app is a window on a screen; the "Lite" version has no desktop. 4 GB of RAM is more than the kiosk needs (it uses well under 1 GB), but it leaves room for the desktop, VNC during setup, and a test run of training if I ever want one, without the Pi swapping memory to the slow SD card.

The 40-pin header

The 40-pin header is two rows of 20 pins along one edge of the board. GPIO means General Purpose Input Output. A GPIO pin is a pin my program can set high (3.3 V) or low (0 V), or read as high or low. Some pins are not GPIO at all; they give fixed power (3.3 V or 5 V) or ground (0 V, the common reference point every voltage is measured from).

Pins have two names. The physical pin number counts positions on the header: pin 1 is at the end farthest from the USB ports, on the row nearer the middle of the board. Odd numbers run along the inside row, even numbers along the outside row at the board edge. The GPIO number (also called the BCM number) is the name the processor chip uses. Physical pin 29 is GPIO 5. My code always uses GPIO numbers, and my wiring notes always give both, because mixing them up is the most common wiring mistake on a Pi.

The names in brackets are second jobs some pins can do. SDA and SCL are the two wires of I2C, a common bus for small sensors. MOSI, MISO, SCLK, CE0 and CE1 belong to SPI, a faster bus. TXD and RXD are a serial port. PWM pins can make a steady on-off pulse pattern in hardware, used for dimming LEDs or driving servos. I kept all of these free so the kiosk can grow later.

Why GPIO 5 and GPIO 6 for the HX711?

The HX711 needs two plain GPIO pins, one input and one output. GPIO 5 and GPIO 6 have no second job (no I2C, SPI, serial or PWM), so using them blocks nothing else. They sit next to each other on the inside row (pins 29 and 31) with a ground (pin 30) right beside them, which keeps the wires short and tidy. Any two free GPIO pins would work; these were the cleanest choice.

Be careful

Pins 27 and 28 (ID_SD and ID_SC) are reserved. Add-on boards called HATs carry a tiny memory chip that the Pi reads through these two pins at start-up to learn what is plugged in. Connecting anything else there can confuse the start-up. I never use them.

The three weak points

The Pi is a great computer, but three of its properties make it awkward for reading a scale. Each one decided a part of my design.

Weak point 1: no analog input

An analog signal is a voltage that can take any value, such as 1.2734 V. A digital pin only knows "high" or "low". To read an analog voltage, a computer needs an ADC (analog to digital converter), a circuit that measures a voltage and gives back a number. Arduino boards have ADCs built in. The Pi has none at all.

A load cell gives a tiny analog voltage, a few thousandths of a volt. So the Pi cannot read it directly. The HX711 board is the answer: it amplifies the voltage and converts it into a 24-bit number, then sends that number to the Pi bit by bit over two digital wires. Chapter 3 explains the HX711 and chapter 12 shows the reading in detail.

Weak point 2: the pins work at 3.3 V

Every GPIO pin on the Pi uses 3.3 V for "high". A pin can be damaged if it sees more than 3.3 V, and the processor has no protection that saves it. The pins can also supply only small currents: about 16 mA from one pin and about 50 mA from all pins together is the safe limit. A milliamp (mA) is a thousandth of an amp.

This is why I power the HX711 from the 3.3 V pin and not from 5 V. The HX711's DT pin sends data to the Pi, and its "high" voltage equals its own supply voltage. If the HX711 ran on 5 V, its DT pin would push 5 V into GPIO 5. Powered from 3.3 V, DT can never go above 3.3 V. The HX711 works fine at 3.3 V; its datasheet allows 2.6 V to 5.5 V.

Be careful

Never connect 5 V to any GPIO pin, even for a moment. Pins 2 and 4 carry 5 V and sit right next to pins 1 and 3. A jumper wire on the wrong pin can kill the processor. I always switch the Pi off before moving any wire, and I check each wire against the table before power-on.

Weak point 3: Linux timing is not exact

Because Linux shares the processor between many programs, my Python program can be paused at any moment, usually for less than a millisecond but sometimes for several milliseconds. Linux is not a real-time system. A real-time system guarantees that a task happens within a fixed time. Linux makes no such promise.

For most of the kiosk this does not matter. A screen update 5 ms late is invisible. But the HX711 has a strict rule: if the clock pin SCK stays high for more than 60 microseconds (60 millionths of a second), the chip thinks it should power down and resets. If Linux pauses my program at the wrong moment during a reading, SCK stays high too long and that reading comes out wrong. Chapter 12 explains this "timing trap" and chapter 13 shows the median filter that removes the wrong readings.

Why not just use a microcontroller for the scale, as in version 1?

A microcontroller has exact timing, so it never hits the trap. But it adds a second device, a second program to flash and a link between the two. In version 1 that link was Wi-Fi through a phone hotspot, and it was the weakest part of the demo. A wrong reading about once every 2 minutes is easy to remove in software. One device with a filter was simpler and more reliable than two devices with a link.

Power needs

The Pi 4 needs 5 V and up to 3 A through its USB-C socket. I used the official Raspberry Pi 5 V 3 A USB-C supply. Phone chargers often give less than 5 V under load, or use thin cables that lose voltage, and the Pi notices.

When the supply voltage drops below about 4.63 V, the Pi shows a small lightning bolt symbol in the top right corner of the screen and slows its processor down to protect itself. This is called under-voltage. It can also cause the camera to disconnect or the SD card to be damaged. If I ever see the lightning symbol, I change the supply or the cable before looking for any other problem.

With the touchscreen, the webcam and the LED strip running, the whole kiosk drew about 1.1 A at 5 V, measured with a USB-C power meter. That is about 5.5 W, well inside the 3 A supply.

SSH and VNC

SSH (Secure Shell) lets me type commands on the Pi from my laptop over the network, as if I had a keyboard on the Pi. VNC (Virtual Network Computing) shows the Pi's desktop in a window on my laptop. I used SSH to copy files and restart the app, and VNC only during setup. The finished kiosk needs neither.

3 The other parts, and why each one

Short version: around the Pi there is a USB webcam fixed 35 cm above the tray, an LED strip for even light, a 5 kg bar load cell under the tray, an HX711 board that turns the load cell's tiny voltage into a number, a 7-inch touchscreen, a 5 V 3 A supply, and the MDF frame from version 1. Every part is there for a reason, and most of the reasons are about making each photo and each weight look the same every time.

The camera: Logitech C270

The camera is a Logitech C270 USB webcam, the same one I used in version 1. It can record up to 1280 x 720, but I use it at 640 x 480. It has a fixed-focus lens, which means it cannot change focus, and it is sharp from about 30 cm to far away. It plugs into a USB port and Linux recognises it with no driver to install. OpenCV reads it as camera number 0.

The camera is mounted 35 cm above the tray and looks straight down. From that height it sees an area of about 29 cm x 22 cm, so the whole 25 cm x 20 cm tray fits in the picture with a small margin.

Why a fixed height and a straight-down view?

A classifier learns what things look like in its training photos. If the camera were at a different height each day, a tomato would appear bigger or smaller, and the network would see sizes it never learned. Looking straight down also means each item is seen from the same angle every time, with no perspective stretching. The version 1 photos were all taken from above, so keeping the same view let me reuse all 636 of them.

Why 640 x 480 and not 1280 x 720?

The network only ever sees a 64 x 64 crop. A bigger frame would cost more time to capture and move around, and almost all of the extra detail would be thrown away by the resize. 640 x 480 gives each item a crop of roughly 100 to 250 pixels across, comfortably more than 64.

The light: a small LED strip

A 30 cm white LED strip is stuck under the camera arm, beside the camera, pointing down at the tray. It runs on 5 V from one of the Pi's USB ports. It is always on while the kiosk runs.

Every webcam has automatic exposure. Exposure is how long and how strongly the sensor collects light for each picture. In a dim room the camera makes the picture brighter, which also adds grainy noise. In a bright room it does the opposite. Colours shift too: yellow room lights make a lemon look orange. With its own strong light close to the tray, the kiosk is lit mostly by the LED strip, whatever the room light is. The camera's exposure settles at almost the same setting every time.

Why does steady light matter so much?

Colour is one of the strongest clues the network uses. Mosambi and Lemon differ mainly in size and shade of green-yellow. If the light changes, the shade changes, and the network's most useful clue becomes unreliable. A Rs 150 LED strip removed a large source of mistakes more cheaply than any change to the network.

The load cell: a bar that bends a tiny bit

A load cell is a sensor that turns force into an electrical signal. Mine is a bar-type load cell: a block of aluminium about 80 mm long and 12.7 mm square, with two holes at each end for screws and a hole cut through the middle. The hole makes the middle thin, so the bar bends in a controlled way when a load presses one end.

It is rated for 5 kg. That means it gives its full signal at 5 kg and is safe up to about 150% of that before it can bend for good. The heaviest item in my list is a coconut at roughly 500 g to 700 g, so 5 kg leaves plenty of room for a bag of potatoes.

Strain gauges

Glued onto the thin part of the bar are four strain gauges. A strain gauge is a very thin zigzag of metal foil on a plastic film. When the bar bends, the foil on the stretched side gets longer and thinner, so its electrical resistance goes up slightly. Resistance is how strongly something resists the flow of electric current, measured in ohms. The foil on the squeezed side gets shorter and fatter, so its resistance goes down slightly.

The changes are tiny. Each gauge is about 1,000 ohms, and the full 5 kg load changes it by only about 1 ohm. Measuring a 1-in-1,000 change directly would be hard. The Wheatstone bridge makes it easy.

The Wheatstone bridge in simple words

A Wheatstone bridge is four resistors joined in a diamond (a square standing on its corner). A supply voltage goes across the top and bottom corners. The output is the voltage difference between the left and right corners.

When all four resistors are equal, the left and right corners sit at exactly half the supply voltage. Their difference is zero. When the bar bends, two gauges go up and two go down, arranged so that one side corner rises a little and the other falls a little. Now there is a small difference between them. The difference is proportional to the bending, and the bending is proportional to the weight.

The analogy I use is a see-saw with two children of equal weight. It sits level. Put a coin on one end and it tips slightly. The bridge does not measure the big weight of the children (the supply voltage), only the small tip caused by the coin (the load). That is why it can sense a 1-in-1,000 change.

My load cell has a rated output of 1 mV per volt. With about 3 V across the bridge, the full 5 kg gives about 3 mV (3 thousandths of a volt). A 412 g tomato gives about 0.25 mV. That is the size of signal the HX711 has to measure.

Be careful

Each bar load cell has an arrow on its side showing the direction the force must push. If it is mounted upside down, the weight still changes the reading but in the wrong direction, so adding an item makes the number go down. I also never press the free end hard with my hand; a sudden push above the rated load can bend the bar for good.

The HX711 board: amplifier and converter

The HX711 is a chip made for load cells. I use it on a small green breakout board, a little circuit board that puts the chip and its few support parts on easy pins. The board has four pins on one side for the Pi (VCC, GND, DT, SCK) and six on the other side for load cells (E+, E-, A-, A+, B-, B+). I use only channel A.

The chip does three jobs.

  1. It supplies the bridge. The E+ and E- pins put a steady voltage across the load cell, about 3 V when the board runs from the Pi's 3.3 V pin.
  2. It amplifies. An amplifier makes a small voltage bigger. With gain 128, the 0.25 mV from a tomato becomes about 32 mV. Gain is the "times" number of the amplifier.
  3. It converts. Its ADC (analog to digital converter) measures the amplified voltage and gives a 24-bit number. 24 bits means 224 = 16,777,216 possible values, from -8,388,608 to +8,388,607. This is very fine: one step is a few billionths of a volt at the input.

The chip then sends the number to the Pi one bit at a time over two wires. DT (data) is the wire the HX711 uses to send bits. SCK (serial clock) is the wire the Pi pulses to ask for each bit. Chapter 12 shows exactly how one reading works.

Why does the HX711 exist at all?

The Pi has no ADC, and even boards that do have one (such as an Arduino with its 10-bit ADC over 0 to 5 V, about 5 mV per step) could not see a 0.25 mV signal at all. The load cell needs a big gain and a very fine converter, both tuned for slow, steady signals. The HX711 does exactly that for about Rs 60, and it needs only two digital pins. That is why almost every hobby scale uses it.

The HX711 can make 10 or 80 readings per second, chosen by a pin on the board called RATE. My board came with RATE tied low, giving 10 readings per second, and I left it that way. Slower readings are less noisy because the chip averages over a longer time inside. 10 per second is also exactly the speed of my app's tick, so each tick gets one new reading.

The touchscreen

The screen is the official Raspberry Pi 7-inch touch display. It shows 800 x 480 pixels and has a capacitive touch layer. Capacitive means it senses the small electric charge of a finger, like a phone screen, so a light tap works and no pen is needed. It connects with a flat DSI ribbon cable and takes its power from two jumper wires on the Pi's 5 V and GND pins.

Why a touchscreen, and why 7 inches?

A kiosk has no keyboard or mouse. Customers expect to tap, as on a phone. Version 1 had a laptop screen and needed a mouse, which nobody wants in a shop. The 7-inch size is big enough for buttons about 2 cm tall and a bill with six or seven lines in a font readable at arm's length. The official screen also needs no driver on Raspberry Pi OS and leaves the HDMI ports and USB ports free.

The power supply

The official Raspberry Pi 5 V 3 A USB-C supply powers everything: the Pi, the screen through the 5 V pin, the camera and the LED strip through the USB ports, and the HX711 through the 3.3 V pin. One plug for the whole kiosk was a goal from the start.

The frame and the tray

The frame is the MDF box from version 1. MDF (medium-density fibreboard) is a cheap, flat, easy-to-cut wooden board. A vertical post at the back holds the camera arm. The blue plastic tray rests only on the load cell, through a spacer, and touches nothing else. Chapter 16 gives the dimensions.

Why a blue tray?

No item in my list is blue. A blue background gives strong colour contrast with every fruit and vegetable, so the item stands out clearly in each photo. It also made the version 1 bounding boxes easy to draw, which helped the crops I cut for version 2.

4 Wiring, exactly

Short version: the kiosk has ten wires I connected by hand. Four go from the load cell to the HX711 board, four go from the HX711 board to the Pi (3.3 V, ground, GPIO 5, GPIO 6), and two give the screen its 5 V power. The camera and the light just plug into USB. Most of this chapter is about the rules behind those wires.

Why screen ground on pin 9 and not pin 6?

The screen's two jumpers are usually put on pins 2 and 6, which sit side by side. In my kiosk pin 6 already holds the HX711 ground, and one pin can only take one jumper socket. All ground pins on the header are joined together on the board, so any ground pin is electrically the same. I used pin 9, the nearest free ground.

I used colour on purpose: red for power, black for ground, yellow for data, blue for clock. When I look into the box, I can check the wiring by colour alone. The webcam and the LED strip go into the black USB 2.0 ports because neither needs USB 3.0 speed, which keeps the faster blue ports free.

The wiring rules, and why each one

Rule 1: the HX711 runs on 3.3 V, not 5 V

Many HX711 guides online connect VCC to 5 V, because they are written for an Arduino, whose pins work at 5 V. On a Pi that would be a mistake. The HX711's DT output swings between 0 V and its own supply voltage. Powered from 5 V, it would push 5 V into GPIO 5, which is built for at most 3.3 V. It might survive for a while and then fail.

Powered from pin 1 (3.3 V), the HX711's DT pin can never exceed 3.3 V. The bridge voltage also drops from about 4.3 V to about 3 V, so the signal is a little smaller. The HX711 still has more than enough resolution, as chapter 13's 431 counts per gram shows.

Why not use a level shifter instead?

A level shifter is a small board that converts 5 V signals to 3.3 V and back. It would let the HX711 run at 5 V with a slightly bigger signal. But it is one more board, four more wires and one more thing to go wrong. Running the HX711 at 3.3 V solves the problem with zero extra parts and costs only a small amount of signal I did not need.

Rule 2: short wires

The four wires between the HX711 and the Pi are 20 cm jumpers, and the HX711 board is screwed to the base right below the Pi. The load cell's own leads are about 25 cm and go straight to the HX711. Long wires act like aerials: they pick up electrical noise from the power supply, the screen and the room. The load cell signal is a fraction of a millivolt, so even a little pickup shows as a jumpy reading. Keeping the analog part (load cell to HX711) short matters most, because after the HX711 the signal is digital and much harder to disturb.

Rule 3: the tray touches nothing but the load cell

The tray's whole weight must pass through the load cell. If the tray rubs against the frame, a cable, or the edge of the box, part of the weight goes into the frame instead. The scale then reads too little, and the error changes every time the tray shifts. I left a gap of at least 5 mm all around the tray and made sure no wire hangs on it.

Rule 4: centre the tray on the load cell

A bar load cell is most accurate when the load sits above its free end, in line with the bar. The tray is fixed to the cell with a spacer at its centre. An item placed at the edge of a 25 cm tray still twists the bar a little, so the reading changes slightly with position. Chapter 13 measures this off-centre error. Keeping the tray centred and not too big kept it small.

Rule 5: a common ground

Ground is the 0 V reference that every voltage is measured from. When the HX711 sends a "high" bit at 3.3 V, the Pi can only understand it if both agree on where 0 V is. That is why the HX711's GND wire goes to a Pi ground pin, even though the HX711 also gets its power from the Pi. The screen's ground goes to the Pi too. Because everything is powered from the one supply through the Pi, there is exactly one ground in the kiosk, which avoids small currents flowing through the signal wires.

Be careful

Jumper wires on header pins are easy to knock loose and easy to put one pin over. After every change I count the pins from pin 1 with a fingertip, and I check that no two jumper sockets are touching. A loose ground on the HX711 makes the readings jump wildly, which looks like a software bug but is not.

Load cell wire colours, and the "weight goes down" swap

My load cell uses the most common colour code: red E+, black E-, white A-, green A+. Some load cells use different colours. If the colours on a cell are not documented, I measure resistance with a multimeter: the two excitation wires and the two signal wires each show about 1,000 ohms across them, and across one excitation and one signal wire it shows about 750 ohms.

If the wires are right but the reading goes down when I add weight, the signal pair is the wrong way round, or the cell is mounted with its arrow upside down. The fix is to swap the white and green wires at the HX711 (A- and A+). This flips the sign of the signal. The bridge supply wires (red and black) stay as they are.

Why fix the sign in the wiring and not in the code?

The code could simply use a negative counts-per-gram number. But then every raw number in my notes and logs would go the "wrong" way, and anyone reading the readings later would be confused. Swapping two wires once makes raw counts rise with weight, which matches every explanation in this book.

Interview tip

Wiring questions are often a quick test of care. My short answer is: "Four wires to the Pi: VCC to 3.3 V on pin 1, ground to pin 6, DT to GPIO 5 on pin 29, SCK to GPIO 6 on pin 31. Design story

In version 1 the HX711 was wired to an ESP8266 (DT on GPIO 4, SCK on GPIO 5), and the ESP8266 sent the weight over Wi-Fi to the laptop. In version 2 the same HX711 board connects straight to the Pi's header. The number of wires from the scale to the computer dropped from "four wires, one microcontroller, one Wi-Fi link and one hotspot" to just four wires.

5 Software setup

Short version: the kiosk uses two machines. My laptop trains the CNN and keeps the photos. The Pi runs Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm 64-bit with four packages from apt (NumPy, OpenCV, Tkinter and rpi-lgpio) and runs the kiosk app. Only one file, weights.npz, moves from the laptop to the Pi. A small systemd service starts the app at every boot.

Two machines, two jobs

Training a network means showing it every training crop 40 times and adjusting 80,604 weights after every batch of 32 crops. That is millions of small calculations, repeated for half an hour. Running the trained network on one photo is a tiny job by comparison. So I split the work: the laptop trains, the Pi predicts.

The laptop keeps the 636 original photos, the cropped dataset and the training code. When training finishes it writes one file, weights.npz, about 320 kB. That file is the only thing the Pi needs from the training side. The Pi has the same layer code (cnn_numpy.py), loads the file, and is ready to predict.

Why not train on the Pi?

It would work, but slowly. The forward pass alone is about 4 times slower on the Pi than on my laptop, and training also needs the backward pass, which costs about twice the forward pass. Forty epochs that take 30 minutes on the laptop would take around two hours on the Pi, while it heats up. Training happens once; predicting happens for every item. So the fast machine trains and the small machine predicts.

Flashing Raspberry Pi OS

Flashing means writing a complete operating system image onto the micro-SD card. I used Raspberry Pi Imager, the official free tool, on my laptop.

  1. Put the 32 GB micro-SD card in the laptop's card reader and open Raspberry Pi Imager.
  2. Choose device: Raspberry Pi 4. Choose OS: Raspberry Pi OS (64-bit), the Bookworm release with desktop. Choose storage: the SD card.
  3. In the settings ("OS customisation"), set the hostname to kiosk, the user name to kiosk with a password, the Wi-Fi network for setup, the time zone Asia/Kolkata, and tick "Enable SSH".
  4. Write the card. Imager checks the written data at the end. Then put the card in the Pi.
Why set things in Imager instead of after boot?

With the hostname, user, Wi-Fi and SSH set in advance, the Pi joins the network on its first boot and I can log in from the laptop straight away with ssh kiosk@kiosk.local. I never need a separate keyboard, mouse or HDMI monitor. The name kiosk.local works because the Pi announces its own name on the local network, so I do not need to know its IP address.

First boot and updates

The first boot takes about a minute longer than usual because the Pi expands the file system to fill the card. Then I log in from the laptop and bring everything up to date.

ssh kiosk@kiosk.local
sudo apt update
sudo apt full-upgrade -y
sudo reboot

apt update downloads the latest list of available packages. apt full-upgrade installs newer versions of everything already installed. sudo runs a command with administrator rights, which installing software needs.

Then I set three options with the configuration tool sudo raspi-config:

  • System Options, Boot / Auto Login, Desktop Autologin: the Pi starts straight into the desktop as the kiosk user, with no password screen. The kiosk app needs a desktop to draw its window on.
  • Display Options, Screen Blanking, No: the screen never goes dark when nobody touches it. A kiosk must always show "Place an item".
  • Interface Options, VNC, Yes: only for setup, so I can see the Pi's desktop from the laptop. I turn it off when the kiosk is finished.

Installing the four packages with apt

The kiosk needs NumPy (fast arrays and maths), OpenCV (camera and image preparation), Tkinter (windows and buttons) and a GPIO library (the HX711 pins). All four come from the operating system's own package list.

sudo apt remove -y python3-rpi.gpio
sudo apt install -y python3-numpy python3-opencv python3-tk python3-rpi-lgpio

Then a quick check that each one imports:

python3 -c "import numpy, cv2, tkinter, RPi.GPIO; print(numpy.__version__, cv2.__version__)"

On my Pi this printed 1.24.2 4.6.0, the versions that come with Bookworm.

Why apt and not pip on the Pi?

pip is Python's own installer; apt is the operating system's installer. On Bookworm, pip refuses to install into the system Python unless forced, to stop pip and apt from overwriting each other's files. The apt packages are already built for the Pi's ARM processor and tested together with this exact Python 3.11, so nothing needs compiling and nothing breaks at the next update. The kiosk needs no newer versions than apt gives, so apt was the simplest and safest choice.

Why rpi-lgpio instead of the classic RPi.GPIO?

RPi.GPIO is the old, well-known Python library for the Pi's pins. It reaches the pins in an old, direct way that newer versions of the Linux kernel (the core of the operating system) and the Pi 5 no longer support. rpi-lgpio is a drop-in replacement: my code still says import RPi.GPIO as GPIO and uses the same functions, but underneath it uses the lgpio library and the kernel's modern, official way of reaching the pins. The two packages cannot be installed together, which is why I remove python3-rpi.gpio first. The same code would also run on a Pi 5.

Be careful

The apt names differ from the pip names. On the Pi it is python3-opencv, but on the laptop with pip it is opencv-python; both are imported as cv2. Mixing them up gives a "package not found" message, not a broken install, so it is easy to fix once I know.

The touchscreen

On Bookworm the official 7-inch display works as soon as its DSI ribbon is connected and its two power jumpers are on pins 2 and 9. There is no driver and no setting to change. The desktop appears on it at 800 x 480 and touch works like a mouse click.

If the picture is upside down because of how the screen is mounted, I rotate it in Preferences, Screen Configuration on the Pi's desktop. Touch rotates with it. If the screen stays black but the Pi boots (I can still SSH in), the ribbon is almost always the cause: it must be pushed fully in at both ends with the latches closed.

Testing the camera

Linux gives each video device a file name: the first camera is /dev/video0. OpenCV opens it as camera number 0. My one-line test opens the camera, reads one frame, prints its shape and saves it.

python3 -c "import cv2; c=cv2.VideoCapture(0); ok,f=c.read(); print(ok, f.shape); cv2.imwrite('test.jpg', f)"

It should print True (480, 640, 3): success, 480 rows, 640 columns, 3 colour values per pixel. Chapter 6 explains this shape. I copy test.jpg back to the laptop with scp kiosk@kiosk.local:test.jpg . and look at it. The whole blue tray should be in view, sharp and evenly lit.

Be careful

The first frame or two from a webcam are often too dark, because automatic exposure has not settled yet. The test above can show a dark picture even when everything is fine. My kiosk code opens the camera once at start-up and reads and throws away a few frames before the first real photo (chapter 15).

The laptop side, for training

The laptop runs Python 3.11 with three packages in a virtual environment. A virtual environment is a private folder of packages for one project, so this project's versions never clash with other projects on the same laptop.

python -m venv .venv
.venv\Scripts\activate          (on Linux or macOS: source .venv/bin/activate)
pip install numpy opencv-python matplotlib

NumPy runs the network maths, OpenCV cuts and resizes the crops, and matplotlib draws the training curves and the confusion matrix picture. There is no PyTorch and no TensorFlow anywhere, on either machine. The training uses only the laptop's processor; no graphics card is needed.

Why the same Python version on both machines?

The Pi runs Python 3.11 and so does the laptop. The weights file is written by NumPy's np.savez and read by np.load, a format that is stable across versions. But keeping Python the same removes one whole class of "works here, not there" problems, and my code only uses features that exist in the Pi's NumPy 1.24.

Getting the code and the weights onto the Pi

The code lives in a Git repository. Git is a tool that keeps the history of every file and lets two machines share the same files. On the Pi I cloned the repository once into the home folder:

cd ~
git clone https://github.com/Gowtham-Jothiramalingam/Self-Checkout-Kiosk.git

After that, git pull inside the folder brings any code change across. The weights file is copied directly with scp (secure copy, which runs over SSH) after each training run:

scp classifier/weights.npz kiosk@kiosk.local:~/Self-Checkout-Kiosk/classifier/

I also keep the final weights.npz and metrics.json in the repository, so a fresh Pi has a working model straight after git clone.

Starting the kiosk at every boot

A kiosk must start by itself after a power cut, with nobody there to type a command. I used systemd, the part of Linux that starts and watches background programs (called services). Each service is described by a small text file called a unit file.

Because the app draws a window, it must run as the kiosk user inside the desktop session, so I made a user service rather than a system-wide one. The file is ~/.config/systemd/user/kiosk.service:

[Unit]
Description=Self-checkout kiosk app
After=graphical-session.target

[Service]
WorkingDirectory=/home/kiosk/Self-Checkout-Kiosk/kiosk
Environment=DISPLAY=:0
ExecStart=/usr/bin/python3 /home/kiosk/Self-Checkout-Kiosk/kiosk/kiosk_app.py
Restart=on-failure
RestartSec=3

[Install]
WantedBy=default.target

Line by line: After=graphical-session.target asks systemd to start the app after the desktop. WorkingDirectory is the folder the app runs in. DISPLAY=:0 tells Tkinter which screen to draw on. ExecStart is the command to run. Restart=on-failure with RestartSec=3 means that if the app ever crashes, systemd starts it again 3 seconds later. WantedBy=default.target means "start this whenever the user's session starts".

Then I switch it on:

systemctl --user daemon-reload
systemctl --user enable --now kiosk.service
journalctl --user -u kiosk.service -f

The first command makes systemd read the new file. The second enables the service for every future boot and starts it now. The third shows the app's printed messages live, which is how I watch the start-up messages and any error without a keyboard on the Pi.

Why Restart=on-failure matters twice

It is a safety net for crashes, but it also solves a start-up race. Very early in the desktop start, the display may not yet accept windows. If the app starts a moment too soon, Tkinter fails to open its window and the app exits with an error. systemd simply tries again 3 seconds later, by which time the desktop is ready. This is simpler and more reliable than guessing a fixed delay.

Measured value (predicted, replace with your own number after your test)

From plugging in the power to seeing "Place an item" took about 35 s on my Pi 4. Most of that is Linux and the desktop starting. The app's own start-up, including the 2 s tare, took about 4 s.

Be careful

The tare at start-up assumes the tray is empty. If someone leaves an item on the tray during a power cut, the kiosk starts with that item as its zero. The app re-tares by itself whenever the tray has been empty and steady for 5 s (chapter 13), so this corrects itself once the item is lifted off, but I still note it in the shop instructions: "Empty tray before switching on."

Interview tip

If asked "how does it start?", I say: "A systemd user service starts the Python app after the desktop logs in. It restarts the app if it crashes. So after a power cut the kiosk comes back by itself in about 35 seconds." Mentioning the restart shows I thought about running it unattended, not just about the demo.

Design story

Version 1 ran from a Python file on a Windows laptop, with hard-coded paths such as a "Downloads" folder in the code and a hard-coded IP address for the ESP8266. Moving to another laptop meant editing the code. Version 2 finds every file relative to its own folder, keeps settings in config.json, and starts by itself, so a fresh Pi needs only git clone, the four apt packages and the service file.
