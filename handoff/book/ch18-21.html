Short version: I built version 2 in ten phases, from buying parts to preparing for interviews. Each phase ended with something I could test on its own before moving on: the gradient check before training, the filter on a laptop before the scale was wired, the app with a simulated scale before the real one. This chapter is that record. Every "What I saw" holds the values from my runs (predicted values, to replace after repeating them).

What I saw: fast and slow convolution agreed; the gradient check passed with a biggest relative difference of 4.9e-07; the 24 random pictures were learned in 60 steps. These three results are real runs.

What I recorded: the gradient check output (chapter 9); the commit "CNN passes gradient check".

Why memorise random pictures before real training?

A network that can learn real data must certainly be able to memorise 24 random pictures. If it cannot, something basic is broken (for example the update step goes uphill), and there is no point waiting 30 minutes for a real run to show it. It takes 10 seconds and catches a whole class of bugs.

  4. Saw the overfitting gap, added augmentation and halved Dense1 to 64 units.
  5. Second real run: 40 epochs, the final settings.
  6. Chose the 0.60 confidence threshold on the validation pile.
  7. Ran the test pile once and wrote down the confusion matrix.

Design stories

Design story: three devices became one

Version 1 needed a laptop, an ESP8266 and a phone hotspot all switched on and connected. At the demo, the hotspot's IP address changed once and the laptop could no longer reach the scale until we edited the code. Every extra device was another thing to power, connect and debug. Version 2's main design goal came from that moment: one box, one plug, no network. The Pi was the smallest computer that could do all three jobs, and every other choice followed from fitting the work onto it.

Design story: detection boxes became classification crops

The obvious move to the Pi was to run a smaller detector. But the kiosk only ever sees one item on a plain blue tray, so "where is it?" is easy and "what is it?" is the real question. I turned the problem around: a colour mask finds the item, and a small classifier names the crop. The version 1 boxes became the tool for cutting 1,850 training crops. A detector of millions of weights was replaced by a classifier of 80,604, small enough to write and check by hand.

Design story: the float money bug

Version 1 added costs as floats rounded to two decimals. While writing the version 2 bill I tested adding 8.78 and 6.3 and got 15.079999999999998. On screen it still showed 15.08, which is exactly why the bug is dangerous: it hides. A rule followed by every system that handles money fixed it: store whole paise and convert to rupees only to print. The half-paisa case then forced a second decision, rounding half up instead of Python's banker's rounding.

Design story: ESP8266 timing versus Pi timing

On the ESP8266 the HX711 never produced a wild reading, because a microcontroller owns its processor. On the Pi, the first 30-minute log showed a reading of thousands of grams every couple of minutes. I considered pigpio and a second microcontroller, then noticed the wild values were always single and enormous. A median of 5 removes them completely, costs 0.2 s, and needs no extra hardware. The problem became a filter design instead of a hardware change.

Two stories from the build

Build story: the gradient check that failed for a good reason

The first time I ran the gradient check, nine of the ten weight arrays agreed to eight digits and conv1 disagreed at the third. I spent an evening rereading the convolution backward pass, which was the same code that passed for conv2 and conv3. The real cause was the measurement: conv1 feeds over 120,000 ReLU units, and a nudge of 1e-5 sometimes pushed one across zero, bending the measured slope. Measuring those few weights again with a ten times smaller nudge gave agreement to six digits. The lesson: when a test fails in only one place and the code is shared, suspect the test before the code.

Build story: the cable that stole 30 grams

During the check-weight test, the 500 g weight read 470 g and drifted by a few grams whenever I touched the camera post. The code had not changed since the calibration, which had been perfect. The camera's USB cable was hanging behind the tray and resting on its back edge, carrying part of the weight into the frame. Clipping the cable to the post brought the reading back to 499.2 g. Since then the first question I ask about any odd weight is "what is touching the tray?"

Interview tip

"Tell me about a bug you found" is almost certain. I pick the gradient check story for software interviews and the cable story for hardware interviews. Both have the shape interviewers like: a clear symptom, a wrong first guess, the real cause, and the general lesson.

When asked "is it finished?", the honest answer is "as a project, yes: every part works and is tested; as a product, no." Then I name the two biggest gaps: the accuracy has not been measured on many customers in a real shop, and the scale is not certified. Saying this first is far better than having it found by the interviewer.

What I would improve, in order

  1. Not-sure handling: already done. Below 0.60 the kiosk shows the top 3 instead of guessing. It is the cheapest improvement and it is in version 2.
  2. Collect kiosk photos. Save every photo with the name the customer finally confirmed. After a few weeks this is a dataset made exactly like the kiosk's own crops, with real labels, to retrain on. It removes the hand-drawn versus colour-mask box difference.
  3. Use the weight as a hint. A 1.2 kg "lemon" is very unlikely; a coconut under 300 g too. Combining the network's chances with a simple weight range per class would fix some Mosambi and Lemon mistakes for free.
  4. Faster settling. Run the HX711 at 80 readings per second in a reading thread and retune the filter, to bring 1.2 s down to about 0.4 s.
  5. Quantised weights. Store weights as 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floats: a quarter of the size and faster on the Pi, with little loss in accuracy.
  6. For a real product: MobileNet with TensorFlow Lite. A pretrained network fine-tuned on kiosk photos would be more accurate and handle more items. This is what I would build for a shop; my from-scratch network was the right choice for learning and for explaining every part.
  7. A certified scale. A legal-for-trade weighing module that sends its weight to the Pi, instead of my HX711.
  8. UPI QR code for the total. Version 1 showed a fixed UPI QR picture; a QR with the exact amount would let the customer pay at the kiosk.
  9. Keep the photo with each bill line. A staff member could check "Wrong item" choices later, which handles the trust problem without slowing honest customers.
Why is the weight hint so attractive?

It uses information the kiosk already has, costs a few lines of code and no extra time, and attacks exactly the most common mistake. A typical mosambi weighs more than a typical lemon. The network sees colour and shape; the scale sees mass. Two independent clues are harder to fool than one.

What I learned

  • Make the problem smaller before making the solution bigger. A blue tray and one item at a time turned detection into classification, and that made everything else possible.
  • Prove the maths before trusting the results. A network with a wrong backward pass still trains, just worse. The gradient check was the only thing that told me my code was right.
  • Split honestly. Splitting by crop would have given me a better number and a false one. The split by photo gave the number I can defend.
  • Hardware problems look like software problems. A loose ground, a touching cable and a Linux pause all showed up as "wrong numbers". Logging the raw readings found each of them.
  • Measure the whole chain. The network was 1% of the customer's wait. Without measuring, I would have optimised the wrong part.
  • Design for the mistakes. A 92.6% model with a one-tap correction is a better kiosk than a model that pretends to be perfect.

21 Interview questions and the one-page cheat sheet

Short version: about 70 questions I expect, grouped by topic, each with a short first-person answer I can say in under 30 seconds. Then a one-page cheat sheet with every number, formula and word I need, and the five things to say clearly.

Interview tip

Answer first, then the reason, then stop. If the interviewer wants more, they will ask, and the next chapters of this book are behind every answer. When I do not know something, I say what I would do to find out.

The big picture

1. What is the project in one sentence?
A self-checkout kiosk for loose fruit and vegetables: it weighs an item with a load cell, names it with a CNN I wrote from scratch in NumPy, and adds a priced line to an itemised bill on a touchscreen, all on one Raspberry Pi 4.

2. Why does this problem exist?
Packed goods have barcodes; loose produce does not, and its price depends on weight. Today a staff member weighs it and keys in the item. My kiosk replaces that step.

3. What are the main parts?
Three jobs. See: USB camera, OpenCV preparation, my NumPy CNN. Weigh: 5 kg load cell, HX711, two GPIO pins, a median and steadiness filter. Bill: a price file, money in paise, a Tkinter touchscreen app.

4. What are your headline numbers?
12 classes, 80,604 weights, 92.6% test accuracy (262 of 283), 18 ms per prediction on the Pi, scale within 2 g from 100 g to 2 kg, about 1.5 s from putting an item down to seeing its name.

5. What was your role?
Version 1 was a team project where I did the GUI, the load cell and the integration. Version 2, on my resume, is my own: the Pi design, the CNN, the training, the scale filter and the app.

6. What does 'edge AI' mean here?
The network runs on the device itself. No photo leaves the box and no internet is needed, so it is fast, private and has no running costs.

Raspberry Pi and hardware

7. Why a Raspberry Pi?
It is the smallest computer that runs Python, NumPy, OpenCV, a USB camera and a touchscreen, and still has GPIO pins for the scale. Its costs are no analog input and inexact timing, which the HX711 and a filter handle.

8. Why not an Arduino or ESP32?
Too little memory (2 kB on an Uno, about 520 kB on an ESP32), no Linux, no NumPy or OpenCV, no easy touchscreen app. The CNN would need a rewrite in C.

9. Why GPIO 5 and GPIO 6?
They are plain GPIO pins with no second job (no I2C, SPI, serial or PWM), they sit next to each other at pins 29 and 31, and a ground is right beside them. Nothing else is blocked.

10. Why is the HX711 powered from 3.3 V?
Its DT output swings up to its own supply voltage. At 5 V it would push 5 V into a 3.3 V GPIO pin. At 3.3 V it can never damage the Pi, and the HX711 works from 2.6 V upwards.

11. What are pins 27 and 28?
ID_SD and ID_SC, reserved for the small memory chip on add-on boards (HATs) that the Pi reads at boot. I never use them.

12. Why does the Pi need an HX711 at all?
The Pi has no analog input. The load cell gives a fraction of a millivolt. The HX711 amplifies it 128 times and turns it into a 24-bit number sent over two digital wires.

13. What does rpi-lgpio do?
It provides the same RPi.GPIO functions my code uses, but implemented on the modern lgpio library and the kernel's GPIO interface, which newer systems and the Pi 5 need. It replaces python3-rpi.gpio.

14. How is everything powered?
One official 5 V 3 A USB-C supply. The screen takes 5 V from pin 2, the camera and LED strip use USB, and the HX711 uses 3.3 V from pin 1. The kiosk draws about 1.1 A.

15. What does the lightning symbol mean?
Under-voltage, below about 4.63 V. The Pi slows down and USB devices can drop out. The fix is the official supply and cable.

16. Why is Linux timing a problem?
Linux can pause my program at any moment. The HX711 resets if its clock pin stays high over 60 microseconds, so a badly timed pause ruins that reading.

Images and OpenCV

17. What does OpenCV do in your project?
Only image handling: read the camera, find the item box by colour, pad, convert BGR to RGB and resize. The recognition is my own NumPy code; OpenCV's neural network module is never used.

18. What is the shape of a camera frame?
(480, 640, 3): 480 rows, 640 columns, 3 colour values from 0 to 255, in BGR order.

19. Why pad to a square instead of resizing?
Resizing a non-square crop squashes the item and destroys its shape, one of the main clues. Padding with the tray colour keeps the true shape.

20. Why 64 x 64?
Small enough for about 2.5 million multiplications and 18 ms on the Pi, large enough to keep shape, colour and coarse texture for 12 items.

21. What is the most important rule about preparation?
Training and the kiosk must prepare images identically. I enforce it with one shared prepare_image function in cnn_numpy.py used by both.

The CNN

22. Why a CNN and not a dense network?
A dense layer on 12,288 pixels would need about 786,000 weights for 64 outputs and would learn every pattern separately at every position. A convolution uses small shared filters, so it has few weights and finds a pattern anywhere.

23. Describe your network.
Input 3 x 64 x 64. Three blocks of 3 x 3 convolution (8, 16, 32 filters), ReLU and 2 x 2 max pooling, down to 32 x 6 x 6. Flatten to 1,152, dense to 64 with ReLU, dense to 12 with softmax.

24. How many parameters, and where?
80,604. Dense1 (1,152 x 64 + 64 = 73,792) holds 92% of them. But the three convolutions do 97% of the multiplications because their weights are reused at every position.

25. How do you compute the output size of a convolution?
Input minus filter size plus 1, with no padding and stride 1: 64 - 3 + 1 = 62. Pooling halves it, rounding down: 29 becomes 14.

26. How do you count conv weights?
Filters x (3 x 3 x input channels) + filters. Conv2: 16 x 72 + 16 = 1,168.

27. What does ReLU do and why is it needed?
It keeps positives and zeroes negatives. Without it, stacked layers collapse into one linear layer and learn nothing extra.

28. What does max pooling do?
Keeps the biggest number of each 2 x 2 block. It quarters the work, tolerates small shifts and lets later filters see more of the picture.

29. What is softmax and what is its catch?
It turns 12 scores into chances that add up to 1. The catch: they always add up to 1, so an unknown item still gets a confident-looking answer.

30. What is the backward pass of max pooling?
The gradient goes only to the position that was the maximum in the forward pass; the other three get zero. I store a mask of the winners.

31. What is the backward pass of convolution?
Same 9-cell loop as forward. The weight gradient is the incoming gradient times the shifted input, summed over batch and positions. The input gradient adds the incoming gradient times the weights back into the same shifted block.

32. How did you make convolution fast in NumPy?
Loop over the 9 filter cells, not the pixels. For each cell, einsum multiplies the shifted input by that cell's weights for all images and positions at once: 9 big operations instead of thousands of small ones. About 120 times faster than the loop version.

33. Explain the einsum string 'nchw,fc->nfhw'.
n picture, c channel, h and w position, f filter. c appears in both inputs and not in the output, so it is multiplied and summed: each filter's output is the sum over channels of input times that cell's weight.

Training and data

34. Where did the data come from?
The 636 labelled top-view photos of version 1. I cut every labelled box into its own 64 x 64 crop, giving 1,850 crops of 12 classes.

35. Why split by photo and not by crop?
Crops from one photo are near-copies: same light, same day. A crop-level split leaks near-twins into the test set and inflates the score. A crop split gave 97.2%; the honest photo split gave 92.6%.

36. What are the three piles for?
Training (1,290) to learn weights, validation (277) to pick the best epoch and settings, test (283) to measure once at the end.

37. What augmentation did you use and why only on training?
Random flip, shift up to 4 pixels, brightness 0.85 to 1.15. It teaches tolerance to small changes. Validation and test must measure the real task, so they are never changed.

38. What loss and optimiser?
Cross-entropy, -ln of the chance given to the right class. SGD with momentum 0.9, learning rate 0.01, batch 32, 40 epochs, He initialisation.

39. Did it overfit?
My first attempt did: 99.6% train, 84.2% validation. Augmentation and halving the dense layer to 64 units brought validation to 93.5% with training at 98.1%. I also keep the best validation epoch, 34.

40. What does 92.6% mean?
262 of 283 test crops from 91 unseen photos were named correctly. Chance is 8.3%. It is a lab number on my own photos, roughly 89% to 96% given the test size, not a shop guarantee.

41. What were the main confusions?
Mosambi and Lemon caused 7 of 21 mistakes; then Brinjal and Brinjal White (4), Onion and Potato (3). Tomato, Coconut and Pomegranate were perfect.

42. How do you know your backpropagation is correct?
A numerical gradient check: for 20 random weights per layer, the central difference with eps 1e-5 in float64 against my gradient. Biggest relative difference about 5e-7. Kinks of ReLU and pooling needed a smaller eps for a few conv1 weights.

43. Why did you drop two classes?
Capsicum had 15 photos and Chilly 17. After a 70/15/15 split there would be about 2 test photos each, which makes their scores meaningless and their learning poor.

Running on the Pi

44. How does the model get onto the Pi?
Train on the laptop, copy the 320 kB weights.npz with scp, and load it into the same cnn_numpy.py layer code on the Pi.

45. How fast is it?
18 ms per forward pass on the Pi 4 (4 ms on the laptop); about 60 ms including a fresh photo, finding the box and preparing it.

46. When does it run?
Once per item, when the weight becomes steady. That is the moment the hand has left and the item is still, and it keeps the processor idle otherwise.

47. What happens when the network is unsure?
Below a top chance of 0.60 the screen says 'Not sure, please choose' and shows the top 3. On the test set the rule caught 11 of the 21 mistakes.

48. Why not use a cloud server?
It needs internet, adds delay, costs money monthly and sends customer photos out of the shop. The Pi does it in 60 ms with none of those.

HX711 and weighing

49. How does a load cell work?
Strain gauges on an aluminium bar change resistance as it bends. Four of them in a Wheatstone bridge give a small voltage proportional to the weight: about 0.25 mV for a 412 g tomato.

50. How do you read the HX711?
Wait for DT to go low, give 24 clock pulses on SCK reading one bit after each, biggest bit first, then a 25th pulse to select channel A with gain 128 next time.

51. How do you turn 24 bits into a signed number?
Two's complement: if bit 23 is set, subtract 2 to the power 24 (16,777,216).

52. What is the timing trap and how did you handle it?
SCK high for more than 60 microseconds resets the HX711. Linux pauses sometimes cause that, giving a wild reading about once every 2 minutes. A median of the last 5 readings removes every one.

53. Why median and not average?
One huge wild value drags an average far away; it can never be the middle of 5 sorted values, so the median ignores it.

54. How did you calibrate?
Tare with the empty tray (123,450 counts), then a 500 g weight (338,950 counts): 431.0 counts per gram, saved in config.json. grams = (raw - offset) / 431.0.

55. Why does a straight line work?
Every step from force to number is proportional, so raw counts against grams is a straight line, fixed by zero and one known weight.

56. When is a weight 'steady'?
When the newest 8 cleaned readings differ by less than 2 g; the billed weight is their average. It takes about 1.2 s.

57. How accurate is the scale?
Within 2 g for 100 g, 500 g, 1 kg and 2 kg check weights, worst +2.0 g at 1 kg. Good for a prototype, not legal for trade.

58. How do you handle drift?
The zero drifts about 1 g per hour. The filter re-tares automatically after the tray has been empty and steady for 5 s.

The app

59. How is the app structured?
A Tkinter window with a tick every 100 ms via root.after. Each tick reads the scale once, updates the filter, and runs a state machine: Waiting, Settling, Identifying, Showing, Remove, plus Choose and Bill.

60. Why not a while True loop?
It would never return to Tkinter's main loop, so the screen would freeze and taps would be ignored.

61. Why keep money in paise?
Floats cannot store most decimals exactly: 8.78 + 6.3 gives 15.079999999999998. Whole paise are exact; I convert to rupees only to print, and round half paise up.

62. What happens if the camera fails mid-sale?
No guesses are made; the Choose screen shows the full list of 12 items, so the customer can still finish.

63. How does it start unattended?
A systemd user service starts it after the desktop logs in and restarts it 3 s after any crash. Power-on to the first screen takes about 35 s.

The honest ones

64. Is it finished?
As a project, yes: every part works and is tested. As a product, no: accuracy is not measured on many shoppers, the scale is not certified, and there is no payment.

65. What was the hardest part?
Two things: getting the convolution backward pass right and proving it with the gradient check, and making the HX711 reliable on a non-real-time Linux system.

66. What would you change?
Collect photos from the kiosk itself and retrain on them, use the weight as a second clue for look-alike items, and read the scale faster to cut the 1.2 s wait.

67. Why write a CNN from scratch when a framework would be better?
For a product I would use a framework and a pretrained MobileNet. Here, writing it myself meant I understand and can verify every number, the Pi needs no heavy framework, and the task was small enough that 80,604 weights reached 92.6%. It was a deliberate learning and engineering choice, not a claim that it beats a framework.
